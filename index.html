The Times of Zheng He

<Narrative Nonfiction>

(Sadlier Unit 1 reading passage — transcribed from textbook photo; boldface words are the Unit 1 vocabulary)

Zheng He was born in the year 1371, in the Chinese province of Yunnan. He was descended from a Persian who had been governor of Yunnan over a century before, in the early days of the Mongol-led Yuan Dynasty. The family retained its connections to the Mongol rulers of China through those years. But nothing is interminable in this world. A change was in the wind. About the time Zheng He was born, the Ming clan asserted itself as the new ruling dynasty in China. Ming armies pushed the Mongols out of China and back to their homeland in Mongolia, an adjacent country in the north, dry and rather barren.

While Zheng He was still a boy, pockets of Mongol loyalists remained in certain areas of China, not least in the southern province of Yunnan. Unrest erupted wherever loyalists lived. Zheng He was about ten years old when political turmoil disrupted his life, forever altering its course. His father was killed in 1381 during the Ming army's conquest of Yunnan. Zheng He was captured and sent to the court of Prince Zhu Di, son of the Ming emperor. If the boy was sullen and withdrawn at first, he did not remain so for long. He became one of the prince's trusted advisors, gradually insinuating himself into a position of respect. In time, Prince Zhu Di sought to depose his own nephew, who had become the second Ming emperor. When the hour came, he relied on Zheng He's assistance. And when the insurrection proved successful, Zhu Di had become the third Ming emperor of China, and Zheng He was now the right hand to the emperor.

This adventure was only a foretaste of what lay ahead for Zheng He. The new emperor had ambitious plans to renovate China after years of vicious war had reduced the population and stalled production to a trickle. The emperor planned to restore the economy, promote construction, maintain order and protect the borders, stimulate culture and learning, and extend China's influence throughout the world. As part of his vision, he sponsored a series of massive naval expeditions, appointing Zheng He to be chief envoy and leader of the fleet.

Zheng He led six expeditions for the emperor, his own prestige increasing with each notch in his résumé. The first expedition set off in 1405, with about 300 huge ships and nearly 30,000 men. At the time, it was the largest fleet ever to have sailed from China.

The main purpose of the expeditions was not exploration, or conquest, or commerce, but diplomacy. Zheng He's giant fleet and the soldiers who manned it made a strong impression wherever they went. They projected an image of Chinese wealth and power. The troops were rarely called on for a display of force; the size of the fleet was intimidating enough. Wherever Zheng He alighted, he had an opportunity to interrogate the locals for news, meet with local elites to exchange gifts and information, and represent the interests of the Chinese emperor.

The emperor died in 1424. His successors did not continue his maritime policy. Zheng He did one more fleet in 1433. He died during this last great expedition and was buried at sea. Seeds of foreign relations planted during his lifetime germinated even after he was gone.

In Poor Taste
<Letter to the Editor>

To the Editor:
RE: “Fast-Food Ads Target Kids” (Business section, October 29):

Our children are sitting ducks in an advertising war. The “combatants” are the food and beverage companies, advertising agencies, and media corporations pelting kids with a dangerous diet of junk food and soft drink ads.

I am no troublesome firebrand. I am a nurse at a middle school. I am also the mother of three school-age children. I am writing to express my indignation and frustration at the indifference of those trying to get one over on the public and, specifically, our kids.

Turn on any children’s TV show or visit a website that caters to kids. You’ll be bombarded with cute characters proclaiming how cool it is to eat Brand “X” cereal or Brand “Y” convenience food. Web sites are full of banner ads and interactive games with commercial content. The problem is, most young children don’t have the sophistication to understand the power of persuasive commercials. They think if their favorite animated dragon says eating sugar-laden gooey treats is good for you, it must be so.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, childhood obesity in the United States has nearly tripled in the past 30 years. Being significantly overweight is a dangerous health hazard. Plus, rates for both diabetes and asthma are soaring. Experts say the rise in both is linked to the rise in childhood obesity. And good health is indispensable for a long and happy life. Our youngest generation is poised to become the unhealthiest in history.

Despite these alarming facts, the food industry and their advertisers use every available media outlet to promote and sell their products. Although their marketing techniques don’t break any laws, pushing food and drinks low in nutritional value and high in sugar, salt, and fat is unethical. Instead of encouraging kids to make good food choices, most of these companies use misleading tactics that hinder healthy eating goals. In fact, a recent university study found that, on any given day, one-third of American children and teens eat fast food.

In an ideal world, I’d like all food advertisements aimed at kids banned from children’s programming. But I know that’s not realistic. After all, we’re talking about a multibillion-dollar industry. So I demand that companies involved in the making, marketing, and selling of food be required to be far more transparent in their advertising to children. I also demand that regulating agencies such as the Federal Trade Commission set far tougher policies. It’s painfully clear that self-regulating guidelines for the food industry are not working.

As a nurse and mom, it is customary for me to be concerned about the health of kids. I will keep doing all I can to dissuade them from choosing junk food over healthy food. I will also continue to plague junk-food peddlers and the government officials who mutually benefit from leaving things as they are.

Human beings are, after all, just human. We’ll always have our bad habits. But cigarette advertising has been banned from the airwaves. Alcohol advertising is strictly regulated. So why do we allow advertising for junk food?

K.D. Nettles, R.N.
Yarmouth, Maine

Lunch at Delmonico’s
<Diary Entry>

October 18, 1876

I’ve had little rest since arriving in New York on business. Harriet and I feel at home at the Fifth Avenue Hotel, which has proved a fine and downright respectable establishment.

Today she set off early, in a quick and animated spirit, to meet with a certain ladies’ group downtown. No doubt the whole brood spent the afternoon discussing women’s suffrage. That topic has entirely absorbed my wife’s attention of late, whenever she has not been seething with excitement about going to the theater. I’ve promised Harriet I will indulge myself by joining her along with her sister and Mr. Treadwell at the theater this evening.

The food here in New York City is quite good. It seems the art of cooking, and with it the sport of eating, culminates on this very avenue at Delmonico’s. Of course, I’d heard it said that Delmonico’s is the finest restaurant in the city, or even in the country. But there’s no substitute for having a look at the facts yourself. I’ve been to Delmonico’s at Madison Square three times already for business luncheons. I daresay I’ve managed to verify the rumors.

Everything at Delmonico’s has the luster of elegance and good taste. The walls are lined with great mirrors. The furnishings are fine mahogany. Silver chandeliers hang from a painted ceiling. In the center of the main dining room there is a large fountain surrounded by flowers. This room enjoys a view of Fifth Avenue, and the well-placed diner can see a well-kept lawn and the trees and gardens of Madison Square. If he forgets to look up from his table, he’ll be delighted almost as much by the craft of every miscellaneous detail, from the tablecloth to the silverware. I kept my eye open for an Astor or Vanderbilt to walk in, but I have yet to see one of the many wealthy and powerful men who frequent Delmonico’s.

The menus are entirely in French. I confess at first I was somewhat peevish and put-off at being forced to choose between guessing what I should order and begging the help of some translator. The upright waiters of the establishment have been of some help in this regard, although my New York associates seem almost as well acquainted with the menu. A few constituents of the menu alarmed me—there was one dish consisting primarily of snails. But my companion on that occasion, Mr. Jameson, managed to goad me into trying it. After my initial shock, I found it a delicate and perfectly delicious dish. I was also persuaded to try the delicious Lobster à la Wenberg. I’m told this is a new concoction of the chef’s. I must say, however, that having sampled a great variety of dishes that were new and unique to me, I began last night to hanker for a good piece of beef. The Steak Delmonico I enjoyed for lunch this afternoon was as fine a cut as I’ve tasted anywhere out West. The chef cooks them to the customer’s preference. I ordered mine rare and slightly singed.

In fact, I’ve just returned from that meal, which I took alone. It’s left me in fine spirits (whoever said “the more the merrier” got it wrong). For the moment, I’ve forgotten about the work ahead of me tomorrow. I suppose an evening at the theater with Harriet and the Treadwells will do me some good after all.

Black Death Victims in Florence, Italy [Image: The city of Florence, Italy, was ravaged
by the Black Death in 1348. Records from the time indicate 80 percent of the city's
residents died of the disease in that single year.]

In 1347, a deadly wave of disease began to sweep across Europe at a very fast rate.
The resulting epidemic, which lasted until the early 1350s, killed tens of millions
of people and is widely known as the Black Death. It was the first of several plagues
that struck Europe in the years and centuries that followed.

Fast Facts
- The main form of the disease that resulted in the Black Death is now known as
bubonic plague. It is caused by a type of bacteria known as Yersinia pestis.
- The Black Death began when these bacteria came to Europe aboard merchant ships.
- Fleas that lived on rats have often been blamed for the spread of the Black Death.
Some researchers now believe, however, that the disease was carried by the fleas
that lived on humans.
- Diseases similar to the Black Death still occur in some parts of the world. They
can be treated with antibiotics if they are detected early. There are also vaccines
available that can prevent the illness.

Origins
Historians and researchers are not entirely sure how the Black Death began. Most
believe the bacteria that caused the plague originally came from Asia. For many years,
researchers thought the disease came from China, but it now appears more likely that
it began in the grassland plains of central Asia. Historical accounts show that the
Black Death was almost certainly brought to Europe aboard merchant ships returning
from Asia. The sailors on board these ships were dead or sick when the ships arrived.

Symptoms
Records dating to the time of the Black Death describe tumors as one of the main
early signs of the disease. These usually appeared in the armpits or lower stomach.
As the disease advanced, it caused the skin around these tumors to turn black, which
is why the illness was called the Black Death. Modern researchers now know that these
tumors were actually swollen lymph nodes filled with blood and pus. The sores would
sometimes burst open and bleed. While this was very painful, people whose sores burst
open actually stood a better chance of surviving.

Beyond these black sores, people with the plague also developed chills, fever,
headaches, vomiting, and muscle aches. People suffering from the sickness became very
weak and tired, and were practically unable to move. It is thought that about half of
those who caught this form of the plague died. Death usually occurred within two to
seven days after symptoms first appeared. In some cases, however, people died within
a matter of hours.

The plague germs sometimes moved into the lungs or the bloodstream, which made people
vomit uncontrollably or cough up blood. About 90 percent of those whose lungs were
affected died of their illness. Practically no one who developed the blood-borne form
of the plague survived.

Spread and Death Toll
The Black Death began in October 1347 in what is now Italy. It began after several
merchant ships returning from Asia docked in the port city of Messina on the island
of Sicily. Many members of the ships' crews were already dead and the rest were sick
with the plague. The ships were ordered to be set adrift back into the water, but by
then it was too late. The disease had already begun to spread among the population.

From Sicily, the Black Death followed two paths as it quickly moved onto the European
mainland. One path led into France, Spain, and Portugal, and moved north from there,
reaching England by the summer of 1348. From England, it went into Scotland, Wales,
and Ireland. The second path moved northeast, through central and northern Europe. By
the time the plague ended in 1353, just about every country in Europe was affected.

The Black Death is often thought of as a disease that mainly affected peasants and the
poor. While this is true, many wealthy people and members of the nobility also died
from it. There is also a misconception that the Black Death only struck larger towns
and cities. In reality, it also reached many areas of the countryside. However, some
parts of Europe were lucky enough not to suffer outbreaks. The plague is known to
have devastated the population of one town while passing over nearby cities and towns.
Researchers are not sure why some areas were hit very hard while others escaped almost
entirely.

In total, the Black Death is thought to have resulted in between twenty million and
seventy-five million deaths, which represented between 30 and 60 percent of Europe's
population at the time. The epidemic was the deadliest event in European history up
to that point. It took several centuries for population levels to make a full recovery.

How People Reacted
Medieval people did not understand the real causes of the Black Death. At the time,
many believed it was a punishment from God and desperately prayed for the disease to
stop. Others thought that if they punished themselves, God would show mercy and halt
the plague. Some people traveled from town to town whipping themselves in public to
show God how much they were suffering. These efforts failed, as did every other
attempt to cure the disease.

Doctors of the era tried many different treatments, including using raw chickens,
cooked onions, sugary syrups, and herbs. Some people burned large fires in the hope
the flames would burn the plague from the air. Church bells were rung at all hours,
as people believed that loud sounds would also remove the disease.

Some Christians thought Europe's Jewish population was responsible for the Black Death.
They believed it was part of a Jewish plot to destroy Christianity. As word of this
idea spread, large numbers of Jews were rounded up, punished, and even killed. These
events mainly took place in France, Spain, and the Holy Roman Empire of central Europe.

Animals such as rats, cats, and dogs were also blamed. In fact, fleas were largely
responsible for carrying the disease from person to person. During the winter, the
rate at which the plague spread seemed to slow down because fleas are inactive during
the winter season.

Connections: Medieval Understanding of the Causes of the Black Death
In the fourteenth century, Europeans had no idea what really caused the Black Death.
The people of the era proposed many possible explanations, including the following:
- A punishment from God
- Unusual movements of the planets
- Air or water pollution
- A Jewish plot to destroy Christianity
- Animals including rats, cats, and dogs

The End of the Black Death
The Black Death finally began to slow down in 1352, although modern researchers are
unsure why. One of the most likely suggestions is that sick people began to be
quarantined, which helped prevent the plague from spreading. The Black Death
eventually stopped, but reoccurrences of the disease appeared again and again for
centuries. Some of these later epidemics were severe, but became less deadly as time
went on. Later outbreaks of the plague also tended to affect smaller geographic areas.
The last European plague outbreak is believed to have ended in the eighteenth century.

- nobility: A wealthy social class that ranked just below royalty in Medieval society.

TITLE: Artificial Intelligence
Source: Gale In Context: Middle School

Artificial intelligence refers to machines that are capable of intelligent functions in the same way as humans. Artificial intelligence is studied in a number of academic fields such as computer science, philosophy, mathematics, psychology, and engineering. The main goal of artificial intelligence research is to create machines that can learn and problem-solve the way that people can. Early models of artificial intelligence performed straightforward human tasks such as calculating math problems. Modern artificial intelligence technologies have advanced to include automated customer service, autonomous vehicles, and intelligent robotics. Some scientists hope to create a conscious machine capable of free thought and emotion. Others believe such a creation would be unethical.

Fast Facts
- IBM created an artificially intelligent question-answering system named Watson that won the quiz show Jeopardy in 2011.
- In 2014, a Russian-created robot named Eugene Goostman tricked people into thinking it was a real thirteen-year-old boy.
- The first multi-layered neural network, named Cognitron, was created in 1975 and gave machines the power to self-organize.

Background
Ideas about machines with the potential for intelligence have existed for thousands of years. Ancient Greek mythology featured stories about robotic creatures. Modern notions of artificial intelligence are rooted in the writings of classical philosophers. These thinkers tried to formulate a symbolic system to describe human thought. Such philosophical ideas spilled over into other academic spheres, such as math, physics, and computer science. Scholars began to theorize about the possibility of creating intelligence. Researchers from a number of scientific fields undertook multiple experiments between the 1940s and 1960s to test this hypothesis.

Early research in neural networks provided some of the first breakthroughs in the science of artificial intelligence. Neural networks were electrical systems that used interconnected nodes to send and receive signals. They were modeled to mimic the biological process of neurons in the brain. Artificial neural networks operated through algorithms, which helped them recognize patterns and correlations in the data provided. Through continuous processing of this data, the system was constantly learning and could improve its function, much like the brain. These early experiments led to the development of computer technology.

One of the earliest pioneers of artificial intelligence was British mathematician and computer scientist Alan Turing. Turing believed it was possible for machines to learn how to solve problems the way that people do. He created a machine called the Turing Machine, which is considered the predecessor of the modern computer. Turing came up with a number of experiments to test the intelligence of his machines. He also developed the Turing test, a test designed to gauge a machine's intelligence. A machine was deemed intelligent if it was able to exhibit intelligence that was indistinguishable from that of a person.

The field of artificial intelligence was formally established at a conference hosted by cognitive scientists John McCarthy and Marvin Minsky at Dartmouth College in 1956. This gathering saw the first use of the term artificial intelligence, which was coined by McCarthy. The very first artificial intelligence program was introduced at the conference. Known as the Logic Theorist, the program was capable of mimicking the problem-solving skills of a human. The events and discussions held at this conference shaped the future of artificial intelligence for years to come.

Early Advances
Artificial intelligence saw many advances over the next few decades. Scientists improved algorithms and opened the possibility of artificial intelligence performing functions such as spoken language interpretation. The success of these projects convinced the federal government to begin funding artificial intelligence research in natural language processing, abstract thinking, and self-recognition.

The main obstacle to further advancements in artificial intelligence was computer data storage. Early computers did not have the power to store enough information to process complex algorithms. It was not until the late 1980s that computer scientists began to make breakthroughs in computer processing power. Scientists developed a type of machine learning called deep learning. It trained computers to perform human tasks such as identifying images and recognizing speech. Deep learning differed from earlier machine learning methods. Instead of using predefined processing paths to organize and execute data, deep learning taught computers to learn on their own. All that was required was a set of basic parameters regarding the data. The computer would then process the patterns of data in layers and learn from each layer of processing.

Artificial intelligence research funding became more and more privatized in the ensuing decades. Private technology corporations funded their own research projects, eliminating limits set by government funding. As a result, artificial intelligence research boomed. Between the 1990s and 2000s, advances in computer science allowed for great leaps in artificial intelligence development.

Connections: Artificial Intelligence in Fiction
Artificial intelligence has appeared in many works of fiction throughout history. These depictions have greatly informed society's perception of artificial intelligence throughout the years. Intelligent robots, computers, cars, operating systems, and other machines have driven the plots of many science fiction books, films, and television shows. Popular culture often takes one of two approaches to stories featuring artificial intelligence. In dystopian works, all possible things have gone wrong with artificial intelligence, pitting man against machine. In utopian works, humans and artificially intelligent machines live in harmony.

Deep Blue Wins
In 1997, IBM created a chess playing program named Deep Blue. The program was able to defeat world chess champion and grand master Gary Kasparov. This victory showed that artificial intelligence engineers were making major progress toward machines with the capacity for decision making. Further, scientists were also developing artificially intelligent machines capable of understanding and exhibiting emotions. The MIT artificial intelligence lab created a robot named Kismet that was able to recognize and mimic emotions.

By the twenty-first century, research in artificial intelligence was closer than ever to reaching its goals. Advances in computer storage limits rapidly expanded the memory and speed of computers. As computer technologies began to saturate everyday life through mobile technology and smartphones, huge amounts of data became available for use in artificial intelligence development.

Modern Artificial Technology
Modern artificial technology takes many forms. Artificially intelligent machines can be programmed by companies to answer customer questions online and by phone. Artificial intelligence has also given rise to the possibility of driverless vehicles, which are capable of self-navigating roads. The primary goal of many artificial intelligence developers was to simplify many aspects of society. Some artificial intelligence researchers also hoped to create a machine that possessed consciousness. This would mean the machine could think freely and critically and come to decisions without human input. Curie, family of French scientists. Pierre Curie, 1859–1906, scientist, and his wife, Marie Sklodowska Curie, 1867–1934, chemist and physicist, b. Warsaw, are known for their work on radioactivity and on radium. The Curies' daughter Irène (see under Joliot-Curie, family) was also a scientist.

Pierre Curie's early work dealt with crystallography and with the effects of temperature on magnetism; he discovered (1883) and, with his brother Jacques Curie, investigated piezoelectricity (a form of electric polarity) in crystals. Marie Sklodowska's interest in science was stimulated by her father, a professor of physics in Warsaw. In 1891 she went to Paris to continue her studies at the Sorbonne. In 1895 she married Pierre Curie and engaged in independent research in his laboratory at the municipal school of physics and chemistry where Pierre was director of laboratories (from 1882) and professor (from 1895).

Following A. H. Becquerel's discovery of radioactivity, Mme Curie began to investigate uranium, a radioactive element found in pitchblende. In 1898 she reported a probable new element in pitchblende, and Pierre Curie joined in her research. They discovered (1898) both polonium and radium, laboriously isolated one gram of radium salts from about eight tons of pitchblende, and determined the atomic weights and properties of radium and polonium. The Curies refused to patent their processes or otherwise to profit from the commercial exploitation of radium. For their work on radioactivity they shared with Becquerel the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics.

The Sorbonne created (1904) a special chair of physics for Pierre Curie; Marie Curie was appointed his successor after his death in a street accident. She also retained her professorship (assumed in 1900) at the normal school at Sèvres and continued her research. In 1910 she isolated (with André Debierne) metallic radium. As the recipient of the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry she was the first person to be awarded a second Nobel Prize. She was made director of the laboratory of radioactivity at the Curie Institute of Radium, established jointly by the Univ. of Paris and the Pasteur Institute, for research on radioactivity and for radium therapy.

During World War I, Mme Curie devoted her energies to providing radiological services for hospitals. In 1921 a gram of radium, a gift from American women, was presented to her by President Harding; this she accepted in behalf of the Curie Institute. A second gram, presented in 1929, was given by Mme Curie to the newly founded Curie Institute in Warsaw. Five years later she died from the effects of radioactivity. In 1995 Marie and Pierre Curie's ashes were enshrined in the Panthéon, Paris; she was the first woman to be honored so in her own right.

Among the numerous and valuable writings of the Curies are Marie Curie's doctoral dissertation, Radioactive Substances (2 vol., 1902; tr. 1961); Traité de radioactivité (1910); Radioactivité (1935); and her biography of Pierre Curie (1923, tr. 1923). Animal husbandry is the branch of agriculture concerned with raising domesticated animals for harvesting valuable animal products (meat, milk, eggs, fibre, hide) or to train animals to provide labour or services. It involves day-to-day management, feeding, nutrition and health, selective breeding, assisted birth, grooming, marketing, and harvest of products. It covers livestock - mainly cattle, sheep, goats, horses, pigs, and poultry (chickens, ducks, geese) - and extends to aquaculture (fish, shrimp, oysters) and insects (beekeeping, sericulture).

History: The first wild animal domesticated was the dog (more than 17,500 years ago), used as guard animal and hunting companion. Pigs were domesticated in the Near East between 8,500 and 8000 BC; sheep and goats in or near the Fertile Crescent about 8,500 BC; cattle from wild aurochs in modern Turkey and Pakistan around 8,500 BC. Introduction

genetics, scientific study of the mechanism of heredity. While Gregor Mendel first presented his findings on the statistical laws governing the transmission of certain traits from generation to generation in 1856, it was not until the discovery and detailed study of the chromosome and the gene in the 20th cent. that scientists found the physical basis of hereditary characteristics. A brief summary of the basic laws of heredity and the terms used follows.

Sections in this article: Introduction; Evolutionary Mechanisms; Modifications of Mendel's Principles; Basic Laws and Terminology; Bibliography.

Evolutionary Mechanisms

The study of mutations, together with the analyses of population genetics, has been used to explain the mechanism of evolution. The elementary process of evolution is considered to be the changes in the frequency of occurrence of alleles in a population. Mutation, which causes the appearance of new alleles or changes the relative frequency of already existing alleles, is one important mechanism by which evolution occurs. Natural selection, by affecting reproductive success, influences the frequencies of alleles and other genetic variants in successive generations. For example, if the presence of a particular allele makes a homozygous individual unable to mate, the allele may be eliminated from the population.

Genetic drift — the random fluctuation in the frequency of an allele, resulting mainly from the vagaries of chance mating — is also an evolutionary mechanism. Although in large populations drift varies only a little above and below a statistical mean, in small breeding populations an entire generation might, by chance alone, be born with the same genotype with respect to a particular allelic pair of genes, thus leading to either the elimination or dominance of a particular gene. Because fluctuations in the proportions of alleles are more significant in the gene pools of small, isolated breeding populations, genetic drift is a mechanism of species diversity and evolution in such groups.

Modifications of Mendel's Principles

Modification of Mendel's principles developed as knowledge of the chromosomes increased; many discoveries have helped to account for apparent deviations from Mendelian ratios. For example, Mendel's studies emphasized genes that behave independently from one another during transmission to offspring. But we now know that genes are transmitted as constituents of chromosomes, each of which carries many different genes, which sheds light on the tendency of certain characteristics to appear in combination with one another (linkage). It also has been found that some characteristics are sex-linked, i.e., are transmitted by genes carried by the sex chromosomes; and that a non-sex-linked gene inherited from the father may differ in its expression from the same gene inherited from the mother, a phenomenon called "imprinting." Other research has shown that there may be multiple alleles (more than two alternative genes) for a given characteristic: the human blood groups are determined by a combination of several possible alleles. It is apparent that homologous portions of paired chromosomes may be interchanged during meiosis (crossing over) and that the interaction of many genes is responsible for determining many of the traits of individuals. Since the discovery (1953) of the structure of DNA, work on nucleic acids has begun to explain how genes determine life processes by directing the synthesis of proteins. It has also explained mutations as alterations in gene or chromosome structure. It has been found, for example, that mutations in the form of repeated sequences of otherwise normal chemical bases, can grow in length with succeeding generations, in some cases causing diseases (e.g., myotonic muscular dystrophy) that increase in severity each time they are inherited.

Most of the knowledge of chromosome structure and the behavior of genes has come from studies of the vinegar, or fruit, fly (Drosophila melanogaster), which reproduces so rapidly that many generations can be studied over a short time. The work of T. H. Morgan and his associates on Drosophila was the basis of much of the early progress of genetics in the United States. Certain other small laboratory animals, plants, and microorganisms such as the E. coli bacteria are now used, also largely because of their ability to reproduce rapidly. For obvious reasons human beings are poor subjects for experimental genetic studies; however, much that aids understanding heredity in humans has been learned from the "lower" forms of life. Also, by tracing the appearance of certain abnormal characteristics (e.g., hemophilia, color blindness, and certain mental disorders and anatomical defects) and blood groups through a number of generations the hereditary pattern of these conditions has been established. The increasing ability of scientists to decode genetic information (see Human Genome Project) has led to a considerable expansion of knowledge about the nature and role of genes in humans and other organisms. Application of this knowledge has played an important role in the fields of gene therapy, genetic engineering, and evolutionary studies, and has resulted in a better understanding of the genetic components of disease, physical characteristics, mental illness, and even personality.

Basic Laws and Terminology

The gene is defined as the unit of inheritance. A gene is actually a sequence of DNA contained by and arranged linearly along a chromosome. Each gene transmits chemical information that is expressed as a trait, e.g., tall or dwarf size in the garden pea plant. Each species has a genome, or characteristic set of genes, that contains the total genetic information for an individual organism. In many familiar organisms two genes for each trait are present in each individual, and these paired genes, both governing the same trait, are called alleles. The two allelic genes in any one individual may be alike (homozygous) or different (heterozygous). The chromosomes of animals and plants that reproduce sexually usually exist in pairs; the members of a chromosome pair are termed homologous. In humans there are 46 chromosomes, or 23 homologous pairs. Pairs of genes are borne on homologous chromosomes.

In the process of meiosis, by which ova and sperm are produced, the chromosomes are so divided that each mature sex cell contains half the original number of chromosomes, or one chromosome of each pair, and therefore one gene of each pair. Thus, when the ovum and the sperm fuse on fertilization, the fertilized egg (zygote) receives one allele from each parent. With many pairs of alleles that have contrasting effects (e.g., certain alleles produce different eye color), one is dominant and the other recessive: an individual heterozygous (carrying contrasting alleles) for a given characteristic invariably displays one aspect of that characteristic and not its alternative, although the gene for the aspect that does not appear (i.e., that is recessive) is present. This individual is called a hybrid.

In Mendelian law the offspring — or first filial (called F1) generation — of parents each homozygous for different alleles of a given gene are all hybrids heterozygous for the characteristic governed by that gene and are said to be of the same phenotype, i.e., they are all similar in appearance to the homozygous dominant parent because the recessive characteristic is masked, although their gene composition, or genotype, is different from either parent. A cross of members of the F1 generation produces a second filial (F2) generation of which approximately three fourths show the dominant characteristic and one fourth the recessive. Note however, that great numbers of characteristics are inherited simultaneously and the patterns of transmission of genes are such that offspring strongly resembling one parent in certain traits can resemble the other parent in other traits.

It has also become clear that an individual organism's heredity and environment interact in the manifestation of many traits: a pea plant with a genetic tendency toward tallness will not achieve its full size if deprived of adequate water and minerals for growth. However, true alterations in gene and chromosome structure are the product of mutation and are not produced by environmental conditions, as was postulated by the theory of acquired characteristics. The discovery by H. J. Muller in 1927 of methods for artificially inducing mutations by means of ionizing radiations and other mutagens opened the way for much new genetics research.

Introduction

Turks, term applied in its wider meaning to the Turkic-speaking peoples of Turkey, Russia, Central Asia, Xinjiang in China (Chinese Turkistan), Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, Iran, and Afghanistan. They total about 125 million, and they are distributed from E Siberia to the Balkans. The wide differences in physical appearance and culture among the Uigurs of China, the Uzbeks of central Asia, and the Osmanlis of Turkey (to cite random instances) make it impossible to speak of Turks as an ethnic or racial group. Although Islam is the religion of the majority of Turks, its importance came relatively late. The most significant unifying link among the Turks is the very close relation of their languages, which are marked by great regularity of pattern and clarity of structure. It is probable that many peoples who were unrelated to the original Turks adopted either wholly or in part their speech and their social organization. The Avars were probably Turkic; they and the Magyars certainly had adopted the Turkic tribal organization when they appeared in Europe, and many Magyar words are of Turkic origin.

The Osmanlis

In Asia Minor the sultanate of Konya was taken over, after the Mongol wave had receded, by the emirate of Karamania, but the Osmanli Turks completed the overthrow of the Byzantine Empire. A minor tribe and the last of the Turkish invading peoples, the Osmanli had been assigned (13th cent.) to the border area of the Byzantine Empire by their Seljuk overlords. It was largely this position as guards of a constantly contested frontier that allowed them to develop their highly disciplined organization, which in turn enabled them in the 14th cent. to make themselves masters of the ruins of the Seljuk empire in Anatolia. Their first historic ruler Osman I, gave his name both to the nation and to the dynasty that ruled an empire extending, at one period, from Vienna to the Indian Ocean and from Tunis to the Caucasus. The people of modern Turkey, which was founded after the collapse of the Ottoman Empire in 1918, are called Osmanli Turks. The original Osmanlis had merged at an early stage with the Seljuks, and their descendants mixed extensively with Muslim converts from the many dozens of nationalities that made up their empire.

Successor States

Among the successor states were the Zangid sultanate of Syria, whose ruler Nur ad-Din was known for his victories over the Crusaders; the empire of Khwarazm, which at one time nearly attained the limits of the earlier Seljuk empire; and the sultanate of Rum or Iconium, which comprised a large part of Asia Minor. All the Seljuk states were overrun in the 13th cent. by Jenghiz Khan and his successors, whose hordes comprised both Mongols and Turks and became generally known as Tatars. The Turko-Tatars now living in the nations of the Commonwealth of Independent States are largely descended from the Golden Horde of Batu Khan, as are the Uzbeks, who ruled a vast empire in the 16th cent.

Seljuk Empire

At the beginning of the 11th cent. a great wave of Seljuk Turks, led by Tughril Beg, conquered Khwarazm and Iran. They entered Baghdad in 1055; Tughril Beg was proclaimed sultan. Under his successor, Alp Arslan, the Seljuks conquered Georgia, Armenia, and much of Asia Minor, overran Syria, and defeated (1071) the Byzantine emperor Romanus IV at Manzikert, opening Byzantium (except for a small area around Constantinople) to Seljuk and Turkmen occupation. This irruption was a major factor in bringing about the Crusades, during which a three-part struggle among Christians, Seljuks, and Egyptian Mamluks developed. Alp Arslan's son, Malikshah (reigned 1072-92), ably administered and developed his huge empire; he was a protector of Omar Khayyam, who reformed the calendar at his behest. At the start of the 12th cent. the Seljuk empire began to fragment, and various parts achieved virtual independence. The attacks of the Khwarazm shah led to the final downfall of the empire in 1157.

Early Migrations and Empires

The name Turk was first used by the Chinese in the 6th cent. to designate a nomadic people who had established a large empire stretching from Mongolia to the Black Sea. This empire, which was divided into two independent parts, was forced to accept Chinese sovereignty in the 7th cent. The northern empire regained its independence in 682, and the oldest known Turkic inscriptions are related to it. In succeeding centuries control of the area passed from the Oghuz Turks to the Uigurs and to the Kyrgyz, who were the last Turkic peoples to reside in Mongolia. They, like their predecessors, migrated to the south and west after they were expelled (924) by the Kitai. Other Turkic peoples, notably the Khazars, Cumans, and Pechenegs, played important roles in the medieval history of S Russia and SE Europe. The Turkish groups of the greatest import in the history of Europe and W Asia were, however, the Seljuks and the Osmanli or Ottoman Turks, both members of the Oghuz confederations. The Arab annexation of the area of ancient Sogdiana in the 7th cent. brought the Oghuz Turks into direct contact with the Abbasid caliphate and later with the Persian Empire. The Turks embraced the Sunni Muslim faith and began to migrate to the Middle East. At first they were used as mercenaries by the Abbasids, but soon the Turks became the actual rulers of the empire.

The Industrial Revolution was a period during the 1700s and 1800s when the methods by which people created material goods changed greatly. Prior to this time, most goods were created by hand. The invention of advanced machines allowed production materials (such as cotton) and final products (such as clothing) to be made at faster rates. The Industrial Revolution led to the rise of factories and consumerism in much of the world. It helped shape human history in many ways.

Fast Facts

- The Industrial Revolution began in Britain and spread to the United States. Most other countries lagged behind due to lack of money for business, political turmoil, and other problems.

- Not everyone was happy about the Industrial Revolution. In the 1800s, a group of disgruntled British workers called the Luddites began destroying textile factories. The Luddites felt they were freeing society of oppression by machines.

- During the late 1800s, the United States and Japan became world industrial leaders. However, many other places, such as China, India, and Russia, did not become truly industrialized until the mid-1900s.

Background

Prior to the 1700s, people crafted most products by hand. With most families living in small rural communities, stores and workshops were limited in number and offerings. People had few material wants, and most of these could be met at home. Families often built their own houses using logs, stones, or other easily gathered materials. People also made their own foods, clothing, and simple tools.

Skilled craftspeople made most specialized items, such as metal tools and firearms, in small workshops. Even relatively complex goods were produced by hand, with each part crafted individually. Each finished product was created from start to finish by one person or a very small number of workers.

Because of the great amount of time and skill that went into each item, material goods were generally rare and costly. Advances in technology, however, drastically and permanently changed the means of production.

Beginning of the Industrial Revolution

The first innovations in the Industrial Revolution appeared in Britain's textile industry. Spinning wool and weaving fabric by hand were very time-consuming. The introduction of a simple but ingenious machine called the flying shuttle in 1733 greatly reduced the time and effort required to create good cloth. The creation of the spinning jenny in 1770 further increased textile production, making Britain the world leader.

Although British authorities guarded their new machines, word spread, along with the spirit of innovation. Inventors in the United States began creating their own revolution. Foremost among these inventors was Eli Whitney, who in 1793 introduced the cotton gin. This machine cleaned cotton much faster than any person could. The cotton gin turned the cotton industry in the American South into an international powerhouse.

The next major stride in the Industrial Revolution also originated with Eli Whitney. This innovation was interchangeable parts, a concept that involved workers creating many identical parts during the manufacturing process rather than creating each part individually. Identical parts meant greater standardization and faster production. It also allowed less-skilled people to replace worn or broken parts on goods with ease.

Industrial machines grew increasingly large and powerful. The creation of the steam engine in the later 1700s brought power to many of these great machines. Increased understanding and use of industrial materials such as coal, iron, and steel fed the Industrial Revolution.

Connections: The Second Industrial Revolution

Some historians believe a second wave of industrial innovations in the 1900s may be considered the Second Industrial Revolution. During this time, new production materials and energy sources meant that products could be created more quickly and cheaply than ever. The introduction of plastics and new metallic alloys ramped up production to unseen heights and allowed the creation of increasingly powerful and complex goods. The Second Industrial Revolution helped usher the world into the digital era of mass-produced computer technology.

Development and Effects

New industrial machines and interchangeable parts contributed to the beginning of the factory system. Factories began to replace workshops in many parts of Britain, the United States, and other countries. In factories, groups of workers, generally less skilled than the craftspeople of prior eras, would use machines to produce large quantities of identical products. The supply of goods increased, and their prices fell. Now even common people could afford a variety of mass-produced goods. Consumerism slowly grew into a defining feature of many societies.

The ideals of mechanization spread to other fields as well, notably agriculture. Whereas agriculture had once been an extremely laborious hands-on task, new farming machines made the work much faster and easier. New inventions allowed farmers to plow, plant, and harvest with great speed. At the same time, innovations in transportation, such as the steamboat and railroad, allowed crops and other goods, along with people, to travel with speed and ease.

The rise of factories and the reduction in manpower needed on farms led to a major social shift. Millions of people who had traditionally lived in rural communities moved to cities in search of factory jobs. This caused cities to grow to vast sizes. Although cities offered people many new opportunities, early cities were often overcrowded, dirty, and depressing. In addition, newcomers to cities often scrambled to cope with low factory wages, leading to a variety of ills including child labor.

- The Soviet Union's Red Army suffered more losses than any other national military in World War II, with more than 7.5 million deaths.
- If the United States had not dropped atomic bombs on Japan, the American war plan would have called for a land invasion of the Japanese mainland.
- In 1948 the United States created the Marshall Plan, which delivered billions of dollars of food and other aid to war-torn Europe.

Causes of World War II

World War II began for several reasons. World War I had ended in German defeat in 1918. The Allies, consisting of France, the United Kingdom, United States, and others, forced Germany to sign the Treaty of Versailles as punishment for its role in the war. The treaty required Germany to pay reparations to countries it had harmed during the conflict. These reparations embarrassed Germany and hurt its economy.

Another cause of World War II was the rise of dictatorships in Germany, Italy, and Japan in the decades after World War I. The most central of these to World War II was the rise of Adolf Hitler and his National Socialist German Workers' Party, or Nazi Party, in Germany. Hitler wanted to glorify Germany again. He believed the German people were better than all other people. He wanted to take over Europe so Germans could spread out over more land. At the same time, Hitler sought to eliminate Jewish people, disabled people, and anyone else he thought was inferior.

Hitler built up a large army during the 1930s. In 1939 Germany made a nonaggression pact with the Soviet Union. The countries agreed not to attack each other and to divide control of Poland between them. The German army invaded Poland on September 1, 1939. This event began World War II. The United Kingdom and France declared war on Germany on September 3.

Six Years of War

Hitler waited until the spring of 1940 to continue invading other European countries. By June, Germany had invaded Denmark, Norway, Belgium, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and France. Hitler never tried to occupy the United Kingdom, although German planes heavily bombed London. Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941. Hitler had once been friendly with Soviet leader Joseph Stalin, but he had always intended to invade the Soviet Union. However, the Soviets fiercely fought the Germans over the next several years and prevented them from ever truly occupying Soviet land.

In addition to invading foreign countries, Hitler also arrested Jewish people across Europe. He put them in concentration camps, where they worked without rest or were sent to gas chambers to die. This mass killing of Jews during the war is called the Holocaust.

The War in the Pacific

In 1931, Japan invaded Manchuria in China. Japan was under economic pressure due to the worldwide Great Depression. It needed raw materials so that it could industrialize and become an equal trading partner with the United States and Europe. From Manchuria, Japan attacked other parts of China. While the Soviet Union tried to halt Japanese expansion, most Western nations sought a diplomatic solution, using trade embargoes to put pressure on Japan.

Japan allied with Germany and Italy, and on December 7, 1941, they launched an attack on the American naval base at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii. Congress declared war on Japan the next day, and Germany and Italy responded by declaring war on the United States. As a result, the United States sent troops to the Pacific and to Europe.

The war in the Pacific was difficult and focused on a strategy known as island-hopping. The United States attacked Japanese islands–even very small ones–to secure supply lines and create new air and naval bases. The US Marines played a major role in the campaign, engaging in brutal combat to take and retain very small areas of land.

End of the War

After careful planning, the Allied powers of the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, and other nations invaded Normandy, France, on June 6, 1944. They planned to defeat the German army there and move east to Germany itself. Meanwhile, Soviet troops would fight Germany in the east and move west. The Allies freed prisoners from the Nazi concentration camps as they progressed.

The Allies had surrounded the German capital of Berlin by April 1945. Rather than be captured, Hitler killed himself in his bunker in the city on April 30. Germany surrendered to the Allies in early May. This ended World War II in Europe.

Japan refused to surrender until the summer of 1945. In August, the United States dropped two atomic bombs on the Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The bombs killed about 220,000 people. Japan surrendered to the Allies on September 2, 1945. World War II officially ended that day. With about sixty million people killed around the world from 1939 to 1945 due to the conflict, World War II was the most destructive war in history.

Checks and balances are the rules and regulations behind the separation of powers in the US federal government. Written in 1787, the US Constitution created a democratic government divided into three separate parts: the legislative branch, the executive branch, and the judicial branch. The Constitution includes a system of rules designed to prevent any one of these branches from becoming more powerful than the others. In effect, these rules ensure that the government remains balanced by providing each branch with the tools to check the other two branches and keep them from becoming too powerful. For this reason, checks and balances are an important part of American government that play a key role in protecting and promoting democracy.

Fast Facts
- The idea of including a system of checks and balances as part of the organization of the American government was first mentioned by founding fathers James Madison and John Jay in the Federalist Papers, a series of articles the two wrote between 1787 and 1788 as a way of creating support for the Constitution.
- Because of the role he played in creating the system of checks and balances, the governmental gridlock that the system sometimes causes is known as Madison's Curse.
- The president's veto power is probably the most well-known example of checks and balances in the federal government, but Congress also had veto power for a time in the twentieth century. Congress' veto power gave it the ability to include special measures in a bill that would prevent the president from altering it in certain ways after it was passed. Most of the methods Congress used to exercise this power were found by the Supreme Court to be unconstitutional in 1983.

History
The history of checks and balances starts with the concept of separation of powers, which is the idea that government should be divided into separate branches. The notion of separation of powers was first suggested by the ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle. He suggested that the most successful type of government would be a mixed government that combined the best parts of monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy. Aristotle's ideas about the separation of powers inspired later political philosophers, including English thinker John Locke. In 1690, Locke wrote that to protect the peoples' rights, it was necessary to separate legislative and executive powers in government. Nearly sixty years later, French political philosopher Baron de Montesquieu added to this idea by suggesting that the judiciary, or the court system, should also be separate from the legislative and executive branches of government. When America's founding fathers set to work on writing the Constitution in the late eighteenth century, they followed Locke's and Montesquieu's advice as they built their new democracy. As a result, the American government was designed to include three separate branches with powers that would be kept equal through a system of checks and balances.

Legislative Branch
The legislative branch, which includes both houses of Congress, has many special abilities it can use to check the power of both the executive and judicial branches. When it comes to the executive branch, Congress has the ability to control how much power executive departments and agencies have and how those powers are used. Congress also has the ability to override a presidential veto and pass a bill that the president rejected. When the president puts together a budget proposal, he or she must submit it to Congress for approval. The House of Representatives and the Senate also have some separate responsibilities related to checking executive power. The Senate is responsible for approving or rejecting the president's nominees for cabinet posts. The Senate is also responsible for impeaching the president or other officials when necessary and approving all treaties with other countries. The House, meanwhile, is responsible for bringing impeachment charges against the president or other officials.
When it comes to the judicial branch, Congress is responsible for approving Supreme Court nominees and impeaching judges when necessary. Congress also has the power to create new court systems, do away with existing court systems, change the size of the Supreme Court, and determine which courts can try different types of cases.

Connections: Marbury v. Madison
The judicial branch's most important role in the system of checks and balances is the concept of judicial review. The Supreme Court's power of judicial review got its start thanks to the 1803 Marbury v. Madison case. That historic case centered around William Marbury, a businessman who President John Adams appointed as justice of the peace in Washington, DC, just before he left office. When the next president, Thomas Jefferson, refused to deliver Marbury's commission, Marbury and several other people in the same position filed a lawsuit that eventually reached the Supreme Court. Ultimately, the Court decided that Marbury and the others were entitled to their commissions, but held that it did not have the authority to force Jefferson to deliver them. In the long run, Marbury v. Madison established the idea that it was the court's responsibility to interpret the law. This, in turn, gave the court the power of judicial review and the ability to strike down laws that are found to be unconstitutional.

Executive Branch
The executive branch is responsible for checking the power of the legislative and judicial branches. Its legislative branch checks include the president's ability to veto bills passed by Congress and the ability to recommend possible legislation to Congress. In addition, the president can also pardon people convicted of federal crimes, and grant amnesty to those who may be tried for such crimes. Finally, the executive branch can also attempt to force the legislature to address important issues by calling special sessions of Congress when needed.
The executive branch's ability to check the power of the judicial branch basically boils down to one responsibility: appointing judges. The executive branch appoints all federal judges, including the justices who serve on the Supreme Court. Although Congress must approve these appointments, the president's ability to choose nominees for judicial positions allows him or her the power to influence the way the court system is likely to lean on important issues.

Judicial Branch
Although the judicial branch has only a few ways to check the powers of the legislative and executive branches, the abilities it does have to do so are especially important. Where the legislative branch is concerned, the court system holds the power of judicial review, which means that it can invalidate the laws passed by Congress if they are deemed to be unconstitutional.
The executive branch is also subject to checks from the judicial branch. Most often, this happens when judges in the court system rule that executive actions issued by the president are unconstitutional and unenforceable as law. In the event that Congress brings impeachment charges against the president or another official, the Supreme Court is tasked with presiding over the trial that follows.

Pre-revolutionary France

While America rebelled against a constitutional monarchy — in which the monarch is the head of state but the ability to make laws resides with an elected government — France was ruled by an absolute monarchy, in which the monarch has complete power over the people. The French people were divided into three estates (levels). The first consisted of the clergy, the second, aristos (nobility), and the third included everyone else, from the wealthiest merchant to the poorest farmer. The first two estates made up a tiny part of the population, controlled most of the wealth, and paid no taxes. By the eighteenth century, a great deal of resentment against the church and the nobility existed among the members of the third estate.

Fast Facts

- People identified King Louis XVI after he escaped using coins that had his face printed on them. - The revolutionaries had their own calendar and means of telling time. The National Assembly

When the American Revolution started, King Louis XVI's (1754-1793) government offered secret aid to the colonists in the form of money, arms, and uniforms. The French did not share the colonists' belief in democracy. Rather, they wanted revenge for their embarrassing loss in the Seven Years' War. In 1777, with the war starting to turn against the British, France entered into an open alliance with America.

France's assistance to the Americans was expensive. The monarchy spent more than two billion livres on the American Revolution and still had debts from the previous war. By 1789, the state was virtually bankrupt. The harvests had been poor and much of the country was starving.

The king's ministers tried to remedy matters. Yet, the aristocrats were unwilling to surrender any of their privileges and resisted all attempts at financial reform. With no other option, Louis called for an Estates-General, an assembly in which representatives from the three estates could air their complaints.

It was the first time an Estates-General had been called in more than 170 years. As a result, the structure of the legislative body was not clearly defined. The first and second estates tried to establish rules to their own advantage. They insisted that each estate should have only one vote. Under those conditions, they would win any measure two to one.

The angry third estate broke away and declared itself the National Assembly. After this rebellion, the king ordered that the assembly hall be locked. Undisturbed, the new assembly met in the tennis courts across the street, where they swore to create a national constitution.

Bastille Day

In the meantime, an episode called the "Great Fear" gripped France. Riots swept across the country, fueled by hungry, angry peasants. Rumors spread that the aristos planned to subdue the third estate by force. On July 14, 1789, armed Parisians attacked the Bastille prison, killing the warden and guards. The prison was nearly empty at the time. But the Bastille had previously held political prisoners, often indefinitely and usually without trial. Its fall held great importance for the revolutionaries.

In response, the king legalized the National Assembly and ordered the other estates to participate in it. On August 11, the assembly produced the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which declared all French males equal in the eyes of the law. The assembly then turned its attention to creating a constitution.

War

Over the next two years, many French aristocrats immigrated to England and Austria. In France, they were called emigres and traitors. Abroad, they stirred up anger against the new French government. In June 1791, Louis XVI also tried to escape the country with his family. However, they were recognized before they could reach the border. The assembly forced them to return to Paris and put them under house arrest.

By 1792, Austria and Prussia had formed an army led by emigres who were bent on attacking France and restoring the old administration. To prevent this, France declared war on Austria. The opening battles went horribly for the French. Austrian forces overwhelmed them at Verdun. The desperate Legislative Assembly passed a measure to recruit a larger army. Hoping to be rescued by the Austrians, Louis vetoed the bill.

The invasion increased the anger Parisians felt. They blamed French losses on the constitutional monarchy and on the king in particular. On August 9, revolutionaries stormed the palace, killing all the defenders. The next day, the king's power was suspended.

Answering the call of patriotism, thousands of men joined the French army. In September, the Austrian army was pushed back at Valmy. The victorious revolutionary government abolished the monarchy entirely and declared the First French Republic, led by an assembly called the National Convention. Louis XVI was charged with treason (crimes against one's country). He was convicted and then beheaded by guillotine in early 1793.

The Reign of Terror

The king's death helped push Great Britain and Holland into the war because they feared the revolution would spread to their countries. In addition, people in the western province of the Vendee, who were angered by the military drafts and food shortages, rose up against the new government. Threatened on all sides, the National Convention created the Committee of Public Safety. In September 1793, the committee was given almost unlimited power to prosecute suspected counterrevolutionaries.

The committee's rule, known as the Reign of Terror, horrified the world. Led by Maximilien Robespierre (1758-1794), the committee tried and executed more than 17,000 men and women. The victims included the former queen, Marie Antoinette, and members of the old nobility. Commoners were also executed. The terror did not end until Robespierre himself was executed in July 1794.

Napoleon

After the Reign of Terror, a five-member directory governed the country. The war raged on as France attempted to "liberate" its neighbors from their rulers. After four years, spirits were low, the economy was bad, and the constitution was unstable. In 1799, the directory fell in a military takeover. General Napoleon Bonaparte (1769-1821) established himself as the new head of state. By 1804, he crowned himself emperor. After fifteen years, the French Revolution had shaken off monarchy only to fall into dictatorship.

However, modern archaeologists have found remains suggesting that the conflict really happened on a hilltop in Turkey. - Ancient tales claim that Mycenaean Greek forces won the Trojan War by hiding soldiers in a huge wooden statue of a horse. When the Trojans took the horse into their city, the soldiers leapt out to attack.

The Roots of Ancient Greece The story of ancient Greece begins with the Minoans and the Mycenaeans. The Minoans were the first people to live in the region known today as Greece. Historians are not sure where they came from, but the Minoans arrived in Greece sometime before 2000 BC and settled on the island of Crete. The Minoans had one of the world's great civilizations. They built great structures, had their own language, and traded with other cultures. After some sort of disaster that happened around 1450 BC, however, the Minoans were wiped out.

The Mycenaeans came to Greece from the area around the Black Sea in about 2800 BC. They also built a great civilization, but always lived in the shadow of the Minoans. When the Minoans disappeared, however, the Mycenaeans began to thrive. Mycenaean culture grew and improved until Macedonian barbarians invaded from the north around 1100 BC. After the invasion, the Mycenaean civilization collapsed and the Greek Dark Ages began. During the Dark Ages, most of the advances the Greeks made up to that point were lost.

Archaic Greece Greece started coming out of the Dark Ages around 700 BC. By that time, the Greeks were starting to see themselves as one culture, even calling themselves the Hellenes. One of the main reasons why this happened was religion. Most Greeks believed in a group of gods who lived high atop Mount Olympus. The king of the Greek gods was Zeus. Some of the other gods included Poseidon (Zeus's brother and the god of the sea), Hades (another of Zeus's brothers and the god of the underworld), and Hera (Zeus's wife and the goddess of women and marriage).

Many important things happened during the Archaic era. For one, the Greeks began to trade more with other people in the outside world and increased their wealth and knowledge as a result. For another, two of the many separate Greek communities known as city-states began to grow more powerful than the rest. These were Sparta and Athens. While life in Sparta revolved around strict rules designed to create strong soldiers, Athens became home to the world's first democratic government. Together, Sparta and Athens twice stopped the Persian Empire from successfully invading Greece. While all this was happening, Greece was going through a period of growing interest in science, philosophy, and the arts that led to the beginning of the greatest period in Greek history.

Classical Greece Starting around 480 BC, Greece entered the Classical age, which is usually seen as the golden era of ancient Greece. During the Classical age, Athens became the leading cultural center of the entire world. For two hundred years, the people of Athens made new scientific discoveries, built great temples to their gods, and wrote and performed in the world's first plays. Athens was also the home of great thinkers such as Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle. Perhaps most importantly, Athens's government model of rule by the people introduced the world to the idea of democracy.

Although many good things happened in Greece during the Classical age, it was also a time of war. First, there was another war between the Greek city-states and the Persians. Later, there was a terrible conflict between Athens and Sparta called the Peloponnesian War. In the end, Athens lost the war with Sparta. This defeat brought about the end of Athens's golden era and left Sparta in power. When Sparta was later defeated in key battles in 371 and 362 BC, the Macedonians took control of Greece. In 336 BC, Alexander the Great rose to power and made Greece part of his empire.

Timeline: Selected Events from Classical Greece - 479 BC: Greek armies defeat the Persian forces of Xerxes. - 478 BC: The city-state of Athens forms the Delian League, an alliance of hundreds of Greek city-states. - 427 BC: Birth of the philosopher Plato in Athens. - 384 BC: Birth of the philosopher Aristotle. - 356-323 BC: Life of Alexander the Great.

Hellenistic Greece When Alexander the Great died in 323 BC, the Classical age ended and the Hellenistic age began. Although Greece itself was crumbling at this time, Greek culture spread throughout the Mediterranean and Middle East. This was mostly thanks to the strong Greek influence in the Egyptian city of Alexandria, which had been an important part of the empire of Alexander the Great. The Hellenistic age continued until Greece was eventually taken over by Rome. The Romans borrowed many of the Greeks' ideas and traditions, and Greek culture lived on to influence the way people live even in the modern world.

City of Gold

Long ago in western Africa, bands of traders traveled to the city of Timbuktu to buy goods in exchange for gold. The following account is given by a boy describing his first trip to the famous city. He is with a group of friends in the year 1450.

My father has been carrying gold from our land to sell in Timbuktu since he was young. His father was a gold trader before him, and now I've joined him and my brothers. We joined other travelers with their goods and camels in a caravan. We brought gold to Timbuktu to sell, and now I've come home. I'll tell you about that city of gold, because soon you'll be old enough to go there with your fathers, too. I'm famished—I haven't eaten since morning. Let's enjoy this small repast together while I talk.

I'll start at the end: What a gainful expedition! You saw the great load of goods we brought home. Immense packages of salt, some expressly for our own use, but most for trade here and to the south. We brought back kola nuts to chew on, some palm oil for cooking, and fine cloth for clothing. We profited more than usual, because the buyers of gold wanted more than was available. One man even offered to lend my father gold at interest so we could trade more before having to leave. Father refused, saying, “Lend your money and lose a friend.”

Before the journey, I had imagined that we would take our bags of gold to Timbuktu and trade a handful here and there. What an inept trader I would become, if not for the wisdom of my father! My father is an ingenious man who knows his way around the world. Before we even entered the city, we stopped to rest by the river. We washed and set up a cooking fire. My father left and returned with a wealthy merchant and his men. They brought us much salt in exchange for our gold. My father and this merchant, who's a big man in Taghaza up north, chewed kola nuts and spoke like old friends. Then, the merchant dispatched his men, ordering them back to their camp. Their compliance was instantaneous, and I watched them recede into the distance, carrying gold this time instead of salt. Irked by my idling, my father told me to stop gawking like an oaf and start packing the salt.

We ate a quick meal and doused the fires. At last, I entered the city of Timbuktu. There are people in Timbuktu of every shape, size, and color, and they come from everywhere, wearing all sorts of apparel and speaking many languages. Many speak Arabic, and many speak Mande, like us. There are thousands of people in that city. It's a busy place, but exciting, with massive mosques and palaces and markets.

We exchanged the rest of our gold in the marketplaces. Because there was so much happening, our time in the city went quickly. Now that I am home, I confess that I had some misgivings before I went. I feared thieves would attack our caravan. I wondered if the city might be raided or besieged while we were there. But everything went smoothly on my first visit. What a place, that busy city—I can hardly wait till we return!

Oxygen from the air we breathe may be the most basic of human needs. People can survive for weeks without food and days without water but only several minutes without access to oxygen. The human body, comprised of microscopic cells, requires a constant flow of oxygen to produce the energy necessary to move, grow, maintain, repair, and reproduce. Human beings have sophisticated respiratory systems to make sure we access adequate amounts of this fundamental element.

Our respiratory system includes our noses, mouths, throats, and lungs; the tubes and passageways that connect these organs to one another; and our diaphragm and chest muscles that enable us to breathe. The brain, heart, and bloodstream are also involved in the process of acquiring and circulating oxygen. The brain directs the body's functioning, and the heart pumps blood at varying speeds throughout the circulatory system to ensure that our cells receive the oxygen necessary for survival.

This intricate system serves most of us very efficiently. However, the respiratory system can be damaged by inhaled smoke and toxic debris. A modern sedentary lifestyle that does not include much exercise can also stress the smooth functioning of our life-sustaining systems. Therefore, a clean environment and sufficient exercise are crucial to the optimal health of our respiratory systems and, ultimately, us.

Our respiratory systems are also affected by stress. When we are under duress, our endocrine system, which produces hormones, may secrete additional cortisol. Such stress hormones have many effects, some of which are increasing our heart rate and breathing rate. This means that not only exercise but also stress can make us breathe harder. It turns out that our breathing, heart rate, and stressful thoughts and feelings are more directly connected than many of us realize. Studying the respiratory system can thus lead to understandings of much greater importance than simply how oxygen circulates in the human bloodstream.
